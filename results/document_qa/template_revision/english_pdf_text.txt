Acoustic Detection and Direction Finding of Small Unmanned Aerial Vehicles 
Using Direction Conditioned Coherence Ranking
Abstract—Coherent environmental sources can dominate 
acoustic spatial maps and cause incorrect association of an 
unmanned aerial vehicle with a bearing. This study proposes 
direction-conditioned coherence ranking, which scores four 
steered-response-power candidates using 24 frequency bands 
of directional coherence. A class-balanced ridge model learns 
from background observations and competing directions 
within aircraft recordings. Evaluation uses outdoor 
measurements from a 208-channel planar array, with the 
100 m position reserved for development and the 150 and 
200 m positions held out for testing. Over 600 aircraft 
observations, the mean angular discrepancy from nominal 
reference directions decreases from 14.78 to 6.97 degrees 
relative to the phase-transform baseline. The proposed 
method achieves a detection rate of 85.7 percent and a false-
alarm rate of 1.5 percent over 200 background observations. 
Under a shared detection gate, the proportion of 
observations detected and localized within 10 degrees 
increases from 76.5 to 85.5 percent. The improvement 
primarily reflects fewer large direction errors. These results 
support candidate-conditioned source association under the 
evaluated conditions; generalization remains limited by 
nominal static references, temporally correlated 
observations, one aircraft model, one acquisition day, and 
location-specific background calibration.
Keywords — UAV acoustics, microphone array, direction 
of arrival, SRP-PHAT, spatial coherence, candidate ranking.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Passive acoustic sensing estimates the presence and 
bearing of an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) from its 
radiated sound [1]. In outdoor environments, a microphone 
array also receives wind noise and emissions from vehicles 
and machinery. A spatially coherent component may 
produce a strong array response regardless of whether it 
originates from the aircraft. UAV sensing therefore 
requires both detection of aircraft-related acoustic 
evidence and association of that evidence with a direction.
Generalized cross-correlation and steered response 
power (SRP) combine intermicrophone delay information 
for source localization [2], [3]. The phase transform 
(PHAT) emphasizes phase alignment by normalizing 
cross-spectral magnitudes. Consequently, frequency bins 
with little UAV energy can still influence the spatial 
maximum. Existing SRP extensions address spectral 
reliability, spatial search, and multiple sources [4]. A 
further association problem remains when the UAV 
direction forms a secondary peak: a positive scalar 
detection score applied uniformly to the map leaves the 
maximizing direction unchanged.
Direction-conditioned coherence ranking (DCCR) 
addresses this problem by evaluating frequency-dependent 
evidence separately for each candidate bearing. SRP-
PHAT generates four directions from the observed audio. 
Steered, normalized cross-spectra then describe each 
candidate through 24 coherence-band features. A class-
balanced linear ridge model assigns a score to each feature 
vector, and the highest-scoring candidate supplies the 
estimated bearing and acoustic detection score.
The proposed method combines a direction-
conditioned representation with competing-direction 
training examples drawn from the same UAV 
observations. Its contribution concerns source association 
using established array-processing operations. A held-out 
comparison on 208-channel field recordings applies a 
common detection gate to conventional and ranked 
bearings, separating changes in direction selection from 
changes in alarm decisions. The following sections 
describe the signal representation, model estimation, 
experimental protocol, and limitations of this comparison.
II.
RELATED WORK
Knapp and Carter formulated generalized correlation 
for time-delay estimation [2]. SRP aggregates pairwise 
delay evidence over a spatial search domain [3], [4]. 
PHAT, smoothed coherence transform (SCOT), and 
coherence-based reliability weights differ in their 
treatment of spectral magnitude and phase. DCCR retains 
these established operations as its spatial front end and 
learns an additional candidate-specific score.
Valin et al. integrated noise-dependent reliability with 
beamforming and particle-filter tracking [5]. Donohue et 
al. studied partial phase-transform weighting in reverberant 
and noisy conditions [6]. Brümann and Doclo examined an 
auxiliary-microphone formulation for SRP estimation in 
coherent noise [7]. These approaches improve spatial 
reliability; the present study instead examines whether a 
coherent candidate is associated with UAV emissions.
UAV acoustic localization can exploit rotor-related 
spectral structure. Uluskan evaluated SRP-PHAT around 
drone harmonics [8], whereas Yang et al. combined 
acoustic signal synthesis with neural localization and real-
audio evaluation [9]. DCCR uses fixed frequency bands 
and a compact linear model suitable for the available 
development recordings. No direct comparison with those 
trained neural systems is available. DDL and UaVirBASE 
provide multichannel data for UAV detection or 
localization [10], [11], illustrating the importance of 
documented acquisition geometry and reference 
measurements. The present data support comparison with 
nominal static bearings but lack synchronized trajectories 
for tracking evaluation.
Unlike a detector applied to a single SRP maximum, 
DCCR compares several directions through their steered 
spectral responses. Positive and wrong-direction examples 
from the same UAV observation share the recording 
background and overall gain. Their labels therefore require 
direction-dependent information, although residual 
dependencies on the acquisition conditions may remain.

All 208 channels participate in a fixed set of 1124 
pairs. Pairs are constructed from channel-index offsets 17, 
35, 53, 71, 89 and 103 modulo 208, deduplicated, and 
restricted to baselines of at least 0.06 m. The rule depends 
only on channel order and geometry. The same pair set is 
used by every compared method, so pair reduction is not 
credited as a DCCR-specific accuracy gain.

The sign follows (1): an earlier arrival at the 
microphone closer to the source gives a positive cross-
spectral phase, which steering cancels. A 1257-point grid 
uniformly samples the horizontal direction-cosine disk at 
spacing 0.05. The vertical component is the nonnegative 
square root needed for unit length. The four highest-
scoring grid candidates separated by at least 12° are 
retained. Each is refined in a ±0.05 neighborhood of 
horizontal direction cosines at spacing 0.005, followed by 
a local angular refinement at 0.1° spacing. The angular 
neighborhood spans ±1° in azimuth and ±2° in elevation. 
The resulting bearing is not claimed to have 0.1° physical 
accuracy; that value is only a numerical search step.
The implementation evaluates inverse-FFT correlation 
tables with eightfold interpolation and linear lag lookup. 
Pairwise phase tests at five known upper-hemisphere 
directions produced discrepancies below 0.054°. These 
deterministic checks verify the sign and numerical search, 
not the accuracy of real measurement labels.

IV.
LEARNING AND JOINT INFERENCE
A. Training examples and hard negatives
The two aircraft recordings at the 100 m development 
position provide positive observations. For each 
observation, the nominal reference direction is used as an 
additional training beam. PHAT proposals within 5° of 
that reference are also positive. Proposals at least 15° away 
are hard negatives; proposals between these bounds are 
excluded to limit ambiguous supervision. Background 
observations provide negative proposals. Reference-

steered beams are used only to form training examples. 
They are never inserted into the candidate set during held-
out inference.
The development set comprises 300 aircraft windows 
and 100 background windows. It yields 1779 labeled 
directional examples: 598 positive and 1181 negative. 
There are 781 wrong-direction negatives from aircraft 
windows and 400 proposals from background windows. 
Examples from a given observation remain together in 
model selection. Two grouped folds each hold out one 
entire aircraft recording and one contiguous 10 s half of 
the development background. Because those background 
halves belong to one recording, this internal validation is 
not an independent-background generalization test.

and evaluation location using the same 25 background

ceil((25+1)(1−α)) is 25. Detection requires a score strictly 
greater than the largest calibration score. Equality is 
rejected. This is an empirical operating-point rule: 
temporally correlated, nonstationary background windows 
do not establish the exchangeability needed for a formal 
5% false-alarm guarantee.
All positive windows receive a bearing estimate, 
including those rejected by the detector. Localization 
results therefore do not improve artificially by discarding 
difficult rejected observations. Joint success is evaluated 
separately as detection together with an angular 
discrepancy of at most 10°.
V.
FIELD EXPERIMENT
A. Recordings and reference information
The acoustic measurements were acquired on 25 May 
2026 with a horizontally placed, upward-facing 
CRY8501/SonoCam Pi array and a DJI Air 3S [12]. The 
archive contains nine recordings: two aircraft recordings 
and one background recording at each of three positions. 
Each recording consists of 208 synchronized-acquisition 
channel files at 48 kHz and 16-bit PCM. Actual file 
durations range from 30.30 to 30.50 s, totaling 273.75 s. 
The stored polar microphone coordinates yield a maximum 
radius of 0.1499944 m. Matching channel formats and 
sample counts were verified across all 1872 WAV files, 
and no sample reached the PCM clipping limits. Matching 
headers do not independently establish microphone phase 
calibration or clock-skew accuracy.
Channel-dependent DC offsets are present, motivating 
the uniform centering operation. No channel was excluded 
after examining localization outcomes. The videos and 
device imaging outputs are not estimator inputs or ground-
truth substitutes. Reference azimuths and elevations are 
taken from the acquisition diagrams in Table I. Their 

uncertainty and any within-recording hovering 
displacement are not quantified by synchronized trajectory 
logs. The reported errors are consequently discrepancies 
from nominal static directions.

The first 30 s of each aircraft recording is used; short 
tails are excluded uniformly. For each background 
recording, [0,5) s estimates a noise reference where 
required, [5,10) s calibrates detection, and [10,30) s

calibration windows. P1 results are development 
diagnostics and are not pooled into these totals. The 
availability of a short local background calibration is an 
explicit operating assumption.
B. Baselines and attribution
The principal baselines are SRP-PHAT, SCOT-SRP 
using Cij from (4), and coherence-weighted PHAT. The 
latter multiplies Qij by clip((L|Cij|²−1)/(L−1),0,1), a finite-
snapshot shrinkage heuristic rather than an exact unbiased 
estimator under overlapping Hann frames. All use the 
same geometry, pair selection, analysis band and search 
refinements.
A reference-assisted variant is also retained. It 
estimates background auto- and cross-spectra from the 
designated reference interval, aligns each channel by the 
median current-to-reference in-band power ratio, subtracts 
the scaled background cross-spectrum, and weights the 
residual phase by bounded residual coherence and 
nonnegative excess-power reliability. Exact operations are 
included in the reproduction code. This is a simple 
subtraction baseline, not a reproduction of the adaptive 
estimator in [5]. Its poor development behavior motivated 
retaining a comparison that tests, rather than assumes, the 
benefit of background subtraction.
The no-hard-negative DCCR model uses exactly the 
same candidate features at evaluation. In addition to each 
method's own detector, a paired comparison applies the 
full DCCR detector decision to every baseline bearing. 
This separates changes in source association from changes 
in the alarm threshold. The best possible candidate 
discrepancy is recorded as an oracle diagnostic after 
inference, never as the estimator output.
C. Measures and reproducibility
For estimated and reference unit vectors, the angular 
discrepancy is
(9)
Mean, median, 95th percentile and fractions within 5° 
and 10° summarize localization. The true-positive rate 
(TPR) and false-positive rate (FPR), denoting detection 
and false alarms, use the designated aircraft and 
background windows, respectively. Equal recording 
durations make the aggregate aircraft mean equally 
weighted across four recordings. Adjacent windows 
remain temporally dependent. Window-level binomial 
intervals or an independent-window bootstrap would 
overstate the effective sample size, so the main comparison 
is descriptive and reports the four recording-level paired 
differences explicitly.
Code, model, protocol, feature-cache and input-
manifest hashes were frozen before held-out execution. 
The models were not revised after reading held-out 
outputs. Verification covers array phase conventions, 
cached-feature reconstruction, ridge-equation residuals, 
amplitude invariance, threshold inequalities, complete 
pairing and summary reconstruction. Runtime 
measurements use an Intel Core i7-12700KF workstation, 
Python 3.12.14, NumPy 2.2.6 and SciPy 1.15.3, with 
numerical-library threads requested as one. Wall-clock 
times are collected during the comparison run, not an 
isolated timing benchmark. They exclude file I/O; the 200 
ms acquisition window is additional to computation. 
Baseline timings conservatively include the shared 
spectral-weight builder. The no-hard-negative timing also 
includes shared candidate preparation and is an upper 
bound for its standalone implementation.

C. Ablation and computational cost
Removing wrong-direction training negatives from 
aircraft recordings yields a mean discrepancy of 6.97°, 
compared with 6.97° for the full model; the values agree at 
the displayed precision. The ablation obtains 78.8% TPR 
and 1.5% FPR. The full model detects 41 additional 
aircraft windows at the same false-alarm count. The 
models select different directions in only three aircraft 
windows, all with discrepancies exceeding 10°. Thus the 
measured benefit of hard negatives concerns detection at 
this calibrated operating point, not bearing accuracy; a 
separate localization gain from this component is not 
established.
DCCR has median and 95th-percentile computation 
times of 414.9 and 588.7 ms per observation; the 
corresponding SRP-PHAT values are 227.9 and 331.3 ms. 
These times include resampling and the cross-spectral 
front end, exclude file I/O, and are additional to the 200 ms 
acquisition window. Refining four candidates and 
computing their directional features increases processing 
cost. The current serial implementation cannot sustain five 
observations per second even on this workstation; real-
time operation is therefore not demonstrated.

VII.
DISCUSSION
The ranking formulation changes the quantity being 
optimized. PHAT searches for broadband phase 
agreement, whereas DCCR compares frequency-dependent 
agreement against a learned aircraft representation at each 
proposed direction. Including wrong directions from the 
same aircraft observation exposes the model to examples 
that share the global spectrum, environmental context and 
recording gain with the positive beam. The shared-detector 
comparison in Section VI evaluates the ranking 
mechanism without crediting it for a different alarm 
decision. The hard-negative ablation supports a detection 
benefit at the selected operating point, but provides no 
separate bearing-accuracy gain.
The reduction in mean discrepancy is driven by fewer 
gross departures from the nominal direction. The pooled 
median changes only from 3.49° to 3.47°, while the 95th 
percentile remains 54.16°. In P3 take 2, 33 of 150 
estimates still exceed 10°. Across all aircraft windows, 34 
of the 36 discrepancies above 10° already lack a 
within-10° candidate. Relative to the nominal references, 
the remaining tail is therefore predominantly a proposal-
coverage limitation. Small systematic offsets in otherwise 
stable estimates, and any contribution from aircraft motion, 
cannot be isolated without more accurate, synchronized 
reference measurements.
The field experiment also exposes a limitation of direct 
reference subtraction. A background recording acquired 
earlier is not an exact noise realization for a later aircraft 
recording. Scaling its average spectrum cannot reconstruct 
changing vehicles, wind or other transients. Subtraction 
can attenuate useful bins or leave a misleading residual. 
DCCR avoids subtracting a presumed contemporaneous 
waveform, but its learned spectral signature remains 
sensitive to changes in aircraft type, operating state, 
propagation and sensor response.
Several limits constrain interpretation. First, the test 
includes one aircraft model, one acquisition day and two 
held-out positions. The six held-out recordings are the 
relevant units of evidence; the 800 analyzed test windows 

do not create 800 independent field trials. Second, the 
calibration background comes from the same location as 
its test recordings, so results do not establish calibration-
free transfer to a new environment. Third, the reference 
directions are nominal static values. Hovering motion, 
orientation registration, sound-speed error and microphone 
phase mismatch can all contribute to the reported 
discrepancy. Fourth, the negative material consists of the 
recorded backgrounds and is not an exhaustive collection 
of confusing machinery, aircraft or simultaneous sources.
DCCR also inherits the proposal stage's coverage limit. 
If SRP-PHAT fails to include a useful aircraft direction 
among the four proposals, a ranker cannot recover it. 
Increasing the number of proposals or introducing several 
spatial front ends could improve coverage, but would 
change both computational cost and the training 
distribution. Such changes require new development data 
and a fresh held-out evaluation rather than retrospective 
tuning on the present results.
The evaluated outputs are a bearing and an acoustic-
presence score for a single UAV. Range estimation, 
identity recognition, simultaneous-UAV separation, and 
trajectory tracking lie outside the experiment. Validation 
on further aircraft, acquisition sessions, and calibrated 
trajectories is required to assess transfer beyond the present 
recordings.
VIII.
CONCLUSION
DCCR combines four SRP-PHAT proposals with a 
compact supervised score over 24 directional coherence 
bands. On held-out field recordings at nominal ranges of 
150 and 200 m, mean discrepancy from nominal reference 
directions changes from 14.78° for SRP-PHAT to 6.97°, 
with 85.7% detection and 1.5% background false alarms. 
Under a shared detector gate, joint success within 10° 
changes from 76.5% to 85.5%. The lower mean 
discrepancy primarily reflects fewer large direction errors. 
The hard-negative ablation supports improved detection 
but does not establish an additional bearing-accuracy gain. 
Further work should improve proposal coverage and assess 
generalization across aircraft, acquisition sessions, and 
synchronized reference trajectories.
